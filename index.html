What Shape Is the Universe?

Astronomers answer this with a triangle. Not a small one, but a triangle stretched across most of the observable universe.

1
Notice + Name

A question bigger than the sky

“If you drew a triangle as big as the universe, would its angles still add up to 180°?”

In school we all learn that a triangle's angles add up to 180°. It's usually taught as a fact about triangles. But it's really a fact about the surface the triangle is drawn on. On a basketball the angles add to more than 180°. On a Pringle chip they add to less.

That caught my attention because it means a triangle can work as a measuring tool. Draw one, measure its angles, and you learn the shape of the space you're standing in, without ever seeing that space from the outside. Ants on a ball could work out that they live on a ball.

We live inside the universe and can never look at it from outside. Can we still find its shape? Yes, by drawing the biggest triangle possible.

Naming the pattern

The property we're measuring is called curvature. There are only three basic options for a uniform space:

SpaceCurvatureAngle sum
Flat (Euclidean)K = 0= 180°
Spherical (closed)K > 0> 180°
Hyperbolic / saddle (open)K < 0< 180°
2
Explain the Math

Three geometries, one test

Flat · Euclidean

Straight lines stay straight, parallel lines never meet. α+β+γ = 180° exactly, whatever the size of the triangle.

Spherical · positive curvature

Like the surface of a ball. The sides bulge outward and the angles get bigger: α+β+γ > 180°. "Parallel" lines eventually meet, the way lines of longitude meet at the poles.

Hyperbolic · negative curvature

Like a saddle or a Pringle. The sides pinch inward and the angles get smaller: α+β+γ < 180°. Parallel lines spread apart.

Girard's theorem: the exact rule on a sphere

On a sphere of radius R, the amount by which a triangle beats 180° (its angle excess) depends only on its area:

α + β + γ = π + AreaR2 angles in radians · excess E = (α+β+γ) − π = Area / R² = K · Area, where curvature K = 1/R²

On a saddle-shaped (hyperbolic) surface with curvature K = −1/R², the same rule gives a deficit:

π − (α + β + γ) = AreaR2 so in every case: angle sum = 180° + K·Area (converted to degrees)
The bigger the triangle compared to the curvature, the bigger the error from 180°. A triangle on your desk drawn on the Earth's surface has an excess of about a billionth of a degree. That's why the Earth feels flat to us, and why testing the universe needs a gigantic triangle.

Worked example: a triangle with three right angles

Start at the North Pole. Walk straight down a line of longitude to the equator. Turn 90° and walk along the equator for a quarter of the way around the Earth (90° of longitude). Turn 90° again and walk straight back to the pole.

  • At each equator corner, longitude lines meet the equator at 90°.
  • At the pole, the two longitude lines are 90° apart, so that angle is also 90°.
90° + 90° + 90° = 270°

Check with Girard: this triangle covers exactly ⅛ of the sphere's surface.

How astronomers draw a triangle across the universe

The oldest light in the universe, the cosmic microwave background (CMB), was released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. It's covered in slightly hotter and colder spots.

Physics tells us the real size of the largest common spots: they match the farthest a pressure (sound) wave could travel in the young universe before the light was released. Measurements also tell us how far away that light comes from.

So we have a giant triangle: us at one corner and the two edges of a spot at the other two. We know the triangle's sides, so we can predict the angle at our corner if space is flat. Then we compare that prediction with the angle we actually see in the sky.

  • Sphere-like (closed): light paths bend outward → spots look bigger than 1°.
  • Flat: spots look about 1° wide (about twice the width of the full Moon).
  • Saddle-like (open): light paths spread apart → spots look smaller than 1°.

The cosmic triangle

The result. The Planck satellite (ESA) measured the CMB spots at about 1°, just as a flat universe predicts. Combined with other data, Planck reports a curvature parameter of Ωk = 0.0007 ± 0.0019 verify, which is zero within the measurement error. As far as we can measure, the universe is flat, or so close to flat that a triangle billions of light-years across still adds up to 180°.

The Geometry of Nepal's Flag

Most countries describe their flag with colours and proportions. Nepal's constitution describes it as a compass-and-straightedge construction.

1
Notice + Name

The only flag that isn't a rectangle

Every national flag in the world is a rectangle, except one.

I'm from Bharatpur, Chitwan, Nepal. I grew up seeing our flag everywhere: on schools, at festivals, on the backs of buses. Two stacked crimson pennants with a blue border, a moon in the top one and a sun in the bottom. To me it was just our flag.

Then I learned something I had never noticed. The Constitution of Nepal (Schedule 1) verify doesn't give the flag's size as a picture or a ratio. It gives a numbered list of geometry instructions: draw this line, make it perpendicular, mark off this length, join these points. Our national flag is a geometry proof that anyone with a ruler and compass can rebuild exactly.

Naming the pattern: the flag is built from perpendiculars, parallel lines, equal lengths, and similar triangles, the tools of Euclid's flat-plane geometry. And all those exact steps produce a height-to-width ratio that is an irrational number.

Building the flag, step by step

We call the base length AB = 1 unit. Every other length in the flag is a multiple of it, so the flag can be made at any size and keep exactly the same shape. Use the buttons to follow the construction.

Step 1 / 18

Steps (a)–(w) follow the constitution's wording. Some of its steps are combined here. verify items should be checked against the official text.

The outer shape in seven moves

  1. Draw the baseline AB.
  2. From A, draw AC perpendicular to AB, with AC = AB + ⅓AB = 4/3.
  3. On AC mark D with AD = AB. Join BD.
  4. On BD mark E with BE = AB.
  5. Through E draw FG parallel to AB, starting at F on AC, with FG = AB.
  6. Join CG. The outline is A→B→E→G→C.

The concepts in play

Perpendiculars: AC ⟂ AB creates a right angle at A, so we can use coordinates and Pythagoras.

Isosceles right triangle: AD = AB and ∠A = 90°, so △ABD has angles 90°, 45°, 45°. BD has slope −1 and length √2.

Ratios on a line: BE = 1 out of BD = √2, so E sits 1/√2 ≈ 70.7% of the way from B to D. That's where √2 enters the flag.

Parallels & similar triangles: FE ∥ AB, so △DFE ∼ △DAB (same angles 90°-45°-45°). That gives FE = FD = 1 − 1/√2.

Where the moon and the sun come from

The emblems aren't placed "by eye". They come from the same construction lines:

  • H is ¼ of the way along AB. The vertical line HI (parallel to AC) is the centre line for both the moon and the sun.
  • Moon: J bisects CF. The horizontal JK meets HI at L, and the line JG meets HI at M. The shortest distance from M to BD gives N. Circles centred at L, M, N, and T carve out the crescent and its 8 rays.
  • Sun: U bisects AF. The horizontal through U meets HI at W, the sun's centre. Its two circles reuse the moon's lengths: radius MN (inner) and LN (outer), with 12 rays between them.
  • Border: the blue border's width equals TN, another length taken from the moon's construction.
Nothing in the flag is measured on its own. The moon's radius sets the sun's size, and the moon's construction sets the border's width. Everything traces back to one length, AB.

Why the ratio is irrational

Without the border, the shape is 1 wide and 4/3 tall. That's a rational ratio. But:

  1. E = (1 − 1/√2, 1/√2): √2 comes in through the 45° line.
  2. P, Q, R, S come from intersecting circles (Pythagoras), which adds more square roots nested on top of √2.
  3. The border width TN inherits all of these. At the five corners the border meets at a sharp "mitre", so it sticks out further there: by t/sin(θ/2), which brings in the slope of CG as another root.

My flag: the SFBU "Golden Bridge" flag

Nepal's flag is defined by rules, not by a picture, so I wrote my own rule set in the same style for a flag for SFBU, the university where I study, far from Bharatpur. Like the constitution, every length comes from one starting length, so anyone can rebuild it exactly at any size.

Schedule: Construction of the SFBU Flag

  1. Draw a vertical line AC of the required height.
  2. Complete the square APQC, with AP drawn to the right of A, perpendicular to AC.
  3. Bisect AP at M. With centre M and radius MQ, draw an arc cutting AP extended at B.
  4. From B draw BG perpendicular to AB, equal to AC. Join CG. The rectangle ABGC is the flag, coloured deep navy.
  5. With centres A and P and radius AP, draw arcs meeting at X. Triangle APX is coloured gold.
  6. The segment PQ cuts the flag into a square and a smaller rectangle PBGQ. Keep cutting the smaller rectangle into a square plus a rectangle, five more times.
  7. In each square, draw a quarter circle centred at the corner, joining corner to corner, so that the arcs form one continuous white curve.
  8. Join CB and GP; let them meet at Z. With centre Z, draw a crimson sun of 12 rays: inner radius AC/22, outer radius AC/11.

The squares get smaller forever, but they all fit inside a flag of finite size and close in on one exact point, Z. How can infinitely many steps end at a single spot? And could you calculate Z's position yourself, before reading the answer below?
Show how to find Z

Put A = (0,0), C = (0,1), B = (φ,0), G = (φ,1). Line CB: y = 1 − x/φ. Line GP: y = φ(x − 1). Setting them equal gives x(φ + 1/φ) = 1 + φ. Since φ + 1/φ = √5 and 1 + φ = φ², this simplifies to x = φ²/√5 ≈ 1.1708 and y ≈ 0.2764. Every square in the spiral has a corner on one of these two diagonals, which is why the spiral closes in on their crossing point.

How Our Topics Connect

One of us builds with triangles. The other tests whether the rules for building them are true.

Same triangles, opposite questions

Nepal's flag construction quietly relies on Euclid's flat-space rules:

  • Parallel lines exist and never meet: FG ∥ AB, JK ∥ AB, HI ∥ AC, UV ∥ AB.
  • Triangle angles sum to 180°: △ABD's 90° + 45° + 45°.
  • Similar triangles: △DFE ∼ △DAB, and scaling the whole flag to "the required length" keeps its shape.

Rojan's project asks whether those same rules hold for the whole universe. The answer (Planck: curvature ≈ 0) says yes, as far as we can measure. So the flag's construction would work even at cosmic scale.

On a sphere, similar triangles don't exist: two triangles with the same angles must also be the same size, because the angle excess depends on area. So Nepal's flag could not be drawn "at any size" on a curved surface. Each size would come out a slightly different shape. The flag being the same at every size is a small piece of evidence that the space we draw in is flat.
